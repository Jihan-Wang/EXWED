\begin{abstract}
We give a deterministic $O(n+k^2)$-time algorithm for bounded weighted
edit distance with costs specified by an input integer metric on the
alphabet extended by a gap symbol. Here $n$ is the total input length and
$k$ is the threshold. The algorithm returns the exact distance and an
optimal alignment if the distance is at most $k$. It reports that the
distance exceeds $k$ otherwise. The running time matches the classical
unit-cost bound and does not depend on the largest metric value.

The algorithm replaces positional diagonals with differences in prefix
gap costs. We prove that removing prefixes of equal total gap cost
cannot increase the distance between the remaining suffixes. This
inequality justifies keeping one representative per score and mass
offset. For real metrics whose nonzero costs are at least one, upward
rounding gives a $(1+\eps)$-approximation in $\softO(n+(k/\eps)^2)$ time. Integer gap costs
improve this bound to $\softO(n+k^2/\eps)$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Edit distance is the minimum number of insertions, deletions, and
substitutions needed to transform one string into another. The classical
dynamic program takes quadratic time~\cite{WF74}. Greedy algorithms solve
the bounded problem in $O(n+k^2)$ time~\cite{Ukk85,Mye86,LV88,LMS98}.
Here $n$ is the total input length and $k$ is a supplied threshold.

Weighted edit distance assigns costs to edits according to their symbols.
The dynamic program extends directly, but the greedy argument does not.
At each score, a unit-cost frontier keeps the farthest position on each
positional diagonal. Under symbol-dependent gap costs, a farther position
can leave a larger distance between the remaining suffixes. This can
happen even when the costs form a metric. Keeping only the farther
position can then discard the cheapest completion.

We recover the $O(n+k^2)$ bound for integer metric costs by replacing
positional diagonals with \emph{mass offsets}. A mass offset is the
difference between the total gap costs of the consumed prefixes.
Our main lemma shows that removing
prefixes of equal gap cost cannot increase the remaining distance.
At each score and mass offset, the farthest generated position can
therefore replace all earlier candidates. The metric triangle inequality
bounds the absolute mass offset by the score. Integer costs give
$2c+1$ possible offsets at score $c$ and $O(k^2)$ frontier entries in total.

\subsection{Our results}\label{sec:results}

Let $X,Y\in\Sigma^*$ have total length $n$, and let
$\gap\notin\Sigma$ be a gap symbol. The input metric $w$ is defined
on $\Sigma\cup\{\gap\}$. Inserting or deleting $a$ costs
$w(a,\gap)$. A substitution from $a$ to $b$ costs $w(a,b)$.
We use constant-time cost queries and exact arithmetic. The input symbols
have polynomially bounded integer codes. Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}
specifies the model.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:integer}
Given an integer-valued metric $w$ and an integer threshold $k\ge1$,
a deterministic algorithm computes $\WED_w(X,Y)$ and an optimal
alignment if the distance is at most $k$. It reports that the distance
exceeds $k$ otherwise. The algorithm uses $O(n+k^2)$ time and space.
\end{theorem}

The bound is independent of the alphabet size and the largest metric
value. The algorithm takes linear time when $k\le\sqrt n$.
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} compares it with bounds for general weighted
costs.

The exponent $2$ is optimal under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
(SETH) in the standard word-RAM model. An $O(n+k^{2-\alpha})$-time
algorithm for any fixed $\alpha>0$ would solve unit-cost edit distance in
strongly subquadratic time by setting $k=n$. This would contradict known
SETH-based lower bounds~\cite{BI18,BK15}.

Let $D=\WED_w(X,Y)$. Doubling the threshold and switching to
the quadratic dynamic program gives $O(n+\min\{D^2,n^2\})$ time
(Section~\ref{sec:exact-distance-sensitive}).

For real costs, we round the metric to integers and apply
Theorem~\ref{thm:integer}. Call a metric \emph{normalized} if every nonzero
cost is at least one. Throughout the paper, $\softO$ suppresses logarithmic
factors.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:approximation}
Let $w$ be a normalized real metric and let $k\ge1$ be an integer
threshold. For $0<\eps\le1$, a deterministic algorithm either reports
that the distance exceeds $k$ or returns a value $A$ and an alignment
of cost at most $A$. The returned value satisfies
\[
 \WED_w(X,Y)\le A\le(1+\eps)\WED_w(X,Y).
\]
The algorithm always returns an approximation when the distance is at
most $k$. It uses
$\softO(n+(k/\eps)^2)$ time and space.
\end{theorem}

For normalized real metrics with integer insertion and deletion costs, the
approximation time improves to $\softO(n+k^2/\eps)$.
The substitution costs can remain nonintegral.
Section~\ref{sec:approximation} proves these consequences
and their versions without a supplied threshold.
The exact frontier also applies under weaker substitution conditions
(Section~\ref{prop:gap-compatible}).

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Greedy alignment algorithms support some symbol-dependent substitution
scores~\cite{ZSSM00}. The wavefront algorithm uses score-indexed
frontiers for fixed affine-gap penalties~\cite{WFA21}. For a fixed finite
alphabet and a fixed rational scoring function, Gorbachev and
Kociumaka~\cite{GK25}
obtain quadratic dependence on $k$.

For general normalized weighted costs, Das et al.~\cite{DGHKS23}
gave an $O(n+k^5)$-time algorithm. Cassis, Kociumaka, and
Wellnitz~\cite{CKW23} gave an exact
$\softO(n+\sqrt{nk^3})$-time algorithm for real costs. Their
lower-bound constructions include metric rational costs with
input-dependent granularity. Gorbachev and Kociumaka~\cite{GK25}
gave the integer and approximation bounds in
Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.
Their algorithm for costs in $[0,W]$ uses a hierarchical alignment
data structure and core-sparse Monge matrices. The metric assumption
lets our algorithm use only a score frontier and longest common
extension queries.

Recent randomized algorithms give strongly subquadratic
$(3+\eps)$-approximations for arbitrary metric weighted edit
distance~\cite{MTW26,DKK26}. Mader, Tavasoli, and Wang~\cite{MTW26}
use the savings formulation, a clipped prefix-mass potential, and
per-character charging of lost savings. Das, Kipouridis, and
Kociumaka~\cite{DKK26} state the unclipped potential. Our
prefix-removal proof adds a crossing argument to this charging method.
The resulting equal-mass suffix inequality supplies the dominance rule
for the bounded frontier.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}

\paragraph{Strings and alignments.}
Let $m=|X|$, $r=|Y|$, and $n=m+r$.
Positions are zero-based. We write $Z[i,j)$ for a substring and
$Z[i:]=Z[i,|Z|)$ for a suffix. The symbol $\emptystr$ denotes the
empty string. Write $g(a)=w(a,\gap)$ for the gap cost of $a$.
A match costs zero.

Weighted edit distance $\WED_w(A,B)$ is the minimum cost of an edit
sequence from $A$ to $B$. For a fixed metric, write
$D(A,B)=\WED_w(A,B)$ and $D=D(X,Y)$.
An alignment is a path from $(0,0)$ to $(|A|,|B|)$ in the edit grid.
Its edges are
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
 (i,j)\longrightarrow(i+1,j)
   &\text{at cost }g(A[i])&\text{(deletion)},\\
 (i,j)\longrightarrow(i,j+1)
   &\text{at cost }g(B[j])&\text{(insertion)},\\
 (i,j)\longrightarrow(i+1,j+1)
   &\text{at cost }w(A[i],B[j])&\text{(substitution or match)}.
\end{array}
\]
Each edge requires the indicated symbols to exist.

For metric costs, weighted edit distance equals the minimum alignment
cost~\cite{WF74}.
Every subpath of an optimal alignment is optimal for its endpoints.
The standard dynamic program and traceback compute an optimal
alignment in $O(n+mr)$ time and space~\cite{WF74}.

\paragraph{Gap masses and savings.}
Define
\[
 \mu(Z)=\sum_{p=0}^{|Z|-1}g(Z[p]),\qquad
 \mu_Z(i)=\mu(Z[0,i)),\qquad
 \delta(i,j)=\mu_X(i)-\mu_Y(j).
\]
We call $\mu(Z)$ the \emph{gap mass} and $\delta(i,j)$ the
\emph{mass offset}. Prefix masses are strictly increasing.
The triangle inequalities involving $\gap$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap-inequalities}
 |g(a)-g(b)|\le w(a,b)\le g(a)+g(b).
\end{equation}
The diagonal pairs of an alignment form an order-preserving matching.
Each position occurs in at most one pair. Pairs $(i,j),(i',j')$ with
$i<i'$ satisfy $j<j'$. Conversely, deleting or inserting all unmatched
positions extends any such matching to an alignment.
Deleting all of $A$ and inserting all of $B$ costs $\mu(A)+\mu(B)$.
Pairing symbols $a,b$ replaces their two gap operations by a diagonal
edge and saves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:savings}
 s(a,b)=g(a)+g(b)-w(a,b),\qquad
 0\le s(a,b)\le2\min\{g(a),g(b)\}.
\end{equation}
Thus a matching $M$ between $A$ and $B$ has alignment cost
\[
 \mu(A)+\mu(B)-\sum_{(i,j)\in M}s(A[i],B[j]).
\]
This savings formulation appears in~\cite[Section~3.1]{MTW26}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:potential}
An alignment subpath from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$ of cost $c$ satisfies
\[
 |\delta(i',j')-\delta(i,j)|\le c.
\]
In particular, a path of cost $c$ from $(0,0)$ reaches only vertices
with $|\delta|\le c$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A deletion or insertion changes the mass offset by its signed gap
cost. A diagonal edge pairing $a,b$ changes it by $g(a)-g(b)$.
The absolute value of this change is at most $w(a,b)$
by~\eqref{eq:gap-inequalities}. Summing the absolute changes along
the subpath proves the claim.
\end{proof}

The lemma is the unclipped case of~\cite[Lemma~3.1]{MTW26} and is
also stated in~\cite[Lemma~3.2]{DKK26}. Sellers' string-metric
property~\cite{Sel74} also gives the lemma via the reverse triangle
inequality with the empty string as reference.
Gap masses always use the metric that scores the alignment.

\paragraph{Computational model.}\label{sec:model}
We count cost-oracle queries and exact arithmetic operations.
Each query, addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and
comparison takes constant time. We measure space in cells that store
exact numbers or symbols. Alphabet symbols have integer codes bounded
by $n^{c_{\rm alph}}$ for a fixed constant $c_{\rm alph}$.
String indexing uses constant-time word operations on
$\Theta(\log(n+2))$-bit words that store these codes.
An alignment can be represented by its edits and intervening match-run
lengths. This representation can be expanded in $O(n)$ time.
In bounded approximation bounds, $\softO$ suppresses factors
polynomial in $\log(n+k/\eps+2)$. In distance-sensitive bounds, these
factors are polynomial in $\log(n+2)$. Hidden constants are independent
of the metric and $\eps$.

\paragraph{Longest common extensions.}\label{sec:lce}
Let $\lce(i,j)$ be the longest common prefix length of $X[i:]$ and
$Y[j:]$. Define
\[
 \Ext(i,j)=(i+\lce(i,j),j+\lce(i,j)).
\]
If either suffix is empty, the LCE is zero.

For the LCE index, radix-sort the input symbol codes and replace them
by their ranks.
Then build a suffix array, its
adjacent LCP array, and a range-minimum index on
$X\#_X Y\#_Y$~\cite{KSB06,KLAAP01,BF00}.
Here $\#_X$ and $\#_Y$ are distinct new sentinels.
This gives deterministic $O(n)$-time and $O(n)$-space preprocessing
for constant-time LCE queries. The sentinels stop queries at the original
string boundaries. The index depends only on $X,Y$ and can be reused
across metrics and thresholds.


\section{Prefix-mass dominance}\label{sec:dominance}

The unit-cost frontier groups vertices by their positional offset $i-j$.
This offset does not give suffix dominance for metric weighted costs.
For example, let $g(a)=g(b)=1$ and $g(c)=2$. Let every substitution
between distinct ordinary symbols cost $2$. These costs form a metric
on $\{a,b,c,\gap\}$.
For $X=\mathtt{abbc}$ and $Y=\mathtt{bcac}$, both $(3,2)$ and
$(4,3)$ are reachable at score $3$ and have positional offset $1$.
Their completion costs satisfy
\[
 D(\mathtt c,\mathtt{ac})=1,
 \qquad D(\emptystr,\mathtt c)=2.
\]
Neither vertex admits a match extension. The farther vertex has the
more expensive completion. Their mass offsets are $0$ and $1$.
The weighted frontier can therefore keep both.

The following lemma supplies the comparison at equal mass offsets.
Its proof uses per-character savings charging, as
in~\cite[Lemmas~6.2 and~6.4]{MTW26}. Order preservation lets us
charge all removed pairs to one prefix.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview-prefix-cut} illustrates this argument.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:removal}
For all strings $P,Q,U,V$,
\[
 D(U,V)\le D(PU,QV)+|\mu(P)-\mu(Q)|.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Take an optimal order-preserving matching between $PU$ and $QV$.
Remove all pairs incident to $P$ or $Q$. Let $L$ be their total
savings. The remaining matching aligns $U,V$ at cost
\[
 D(PU,QV)-\mu(P)-\mu(Q)+L.
\]
If a pair connects $P$ to $V$, no pair can connect $U$ to $Q$.
The two pairs would cross. Every removed pair then has its source
position in $P$. Each source position occurs in at most one pair.
Each pair saves at most twice the gap cost of its source symbol
by~\eqref{eq:savings}.
Hence $L\le2\mu(P)$.
If no pair connects $P$ to $V$, every removed pair has its target
position in $Q$. Charging each removed pair to its target symbol
gives $L\le2\mu(Q)$.
In either case, $L\le2\max\{\mu(P),\mu(Q)\}$.
The remaining matching therefore has cost at most
$D(PU,QV)+|\mu(P)-\mu(Q)|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:dominance}
If $i_2\ge i_1$ and $\delta(i_2,j_2)=\delta(i_1,j_1)$, then
$j_2\ge j_1$ and
\[
 D(X[i_2:],Y[j_2:])\le D(X[i_1:],Y[j_1:]).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Equality of the offsets gives
$\mu_Y(j_2)-\mu_Y(j_1)=\mu_X(i_2)-\mu_X(i_1)\ge0$.
Since prefix masses strictly increase, $j_2\ge j_1$.
The intervening prefixes $X[i_1,i_2)$ and $Y[j_1,j_2)$ have equal
mass. Lemma~\ref{lem:removal} gives the distance inequality.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:removal} also gives the known common-prefix cancellation
property~\cite[Corollary~3.6]{CKW23}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:cancellation}\label{lem:extension}
For all strings $P,U,V$, $D(PU,PV)=D(U,V)$.
Applying $\Ext$ therefore preserves both the mass offset and the
remaining distance.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:removal} gives $D(U,V)\le D(PU,PV)$.
Matching the two copies of $P$ for free gives the reverse inequality.
\end{proof}

\section{The bounded weighted frontier}\label{sec:integer}\label{sec:frontier}

For each integer score $0\le c\le k$ and mass offset $-c\le d\le c$,
the entry $R[c,d]$ stores a record for a vertex $(i,j)$ reached by an
alignment of cost exactly $c$ with $\delta(i,j)=d$.
Each entry retains the generated candidate with the largest source
index $i$ after match extension.
Corollary~\ref{cor:dominance} ensures that replacing an earlier
candidate cannot increase the remaining distance.

From a stored vertex $(i,j)$, generate each valid insertion, deletion,
and substitution. Every generated edit has positive integer cost
because match extension has already consumed all immediate matches.
The destination therefore belongs to a later score layer. The
mass-potential bound in Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} places every
candidate of score $c$ within $[-c,c]$.

Each record stores its vertex, predecessor entry $p$, last positive
edit $e$, and the length $\ell$ of the following match run.
The procedure $\Offer(c,i,j,p,e)$ discards a candidate if $c>k$.
Otherwise, it sets $\ell=\lce(i,j)$ and replaces $(i,j)$ by
$(i+\ell,j+\ell)$. It then computes $d=\delta(i,j)$.
The candidate is stored in $R[c,d]$ if the entry is empty or has a
smaller source index. The root stores its initial match run.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:frontier} processes score layers in increasing
order and returns upon processing the first terminal record.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bounded weighted edit distance}\label{alg:frontier}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $X,Y$, integer metric $w$, threshold $k\ge1$, gap costs,
prefix masses, and LCE index
\Function{Frontier}{$k$}
  \State Initialize $R[c,d]$ as empty for $0\le c\le k$, $-c\le d\le c$
  \State \Call{Offer}{$0,0,0,\mathsf{null},\mathsf{null}$}
  \For{$c=0,1,\ldots,k$}
    \For{$d=-c,-c+1,\ldots,c$}
      \If{$R[c,d]$ is empty} \State \textbf{continue} \EndIf
      \State $(i,j)\gets(R[c,d].i,R[c,d].j)$
      \If{$(i,j)=(m,r)$}
        \State \Return $c$ and the alignment from the predecessor chain
      \EndIf
      \ForAll{outgoing edit edges $e:(i,j)\to(i',j')$ of cost $t$}
        \State \Call{Offer}{$c+t,i',j',(c,d),e$}
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\FAIL$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Correctness and running time}\label{sec:traceback}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:integer}]
Every stored record represents a prefix alignment of its stated score.
The root matches a common prefix. Each later record appends one edit
and a match run to its predecessor's alignment.
Positive edits increase the score. Thus a layer is final before it is
processed. Its records cannot change after they generate children.
The predecessor pointers therefore remain valid.

Let $D=\WED_w(X,Y)$ and suppose $D\le k$. Call a record at
score $c$ and vertex $(i,j)$ \emph{feasible} if
\[
 c+D(X[i:],Y[j:])\le D.
\]
A feasible record can be completed to an optimal alignment. It suffices
to preserve one such record as the algorithm processes the score layers.
The initial record is feasible by common-prefix cancellation.
Match extension preserves feasibility. Replacing a candidate by a later
vertex at the same score and mass offset also preserves feasibility
by Corollary~\ref{cor:dominance}.

At a feasible nonterminal record, match extension leaves no immediate match.
An optimal suffix alignment therefore starts with a positive edit.
Let this edit have cost $t$ and end at $(i',j')$.
Then
\[
 t+D(X[i':],Y[j':])=D(X[i:],Y[j:]).
\]
The algorithm generates this edit at score $c+t\le D\le k$.
Match extension and comparison produce a feasible record in a later
layer. Subsequent replacements preserve feasibility until that layer
is processed. Following these feasible representatives gives strictly
increasing integer scores bounded by $D$. The sequence must therefore
reach a terminal record. Every terminal record realizes an alignment
and has score at least $D$. The first processed terminal score is
therefore $D$.
If $D>k$, no terminal record can occur in the processed layers.

At score $c$ there are $2c+1$ possible offsets.
The frontier therefore has $O(k^2)$ entries.
Each entry is processed once and generates at most
three candidates. Each candidate uses one LCE query and constant
additional work. Gap costs, prefix masses, and the LCE index take
$O(n)$ preprocessing time and space. Thus the total time and space
are $O(n+k^2)$.

Follow the terminal record's predecessor chain and reverse its blocks
$(e,\ell)$ to obtain the alignment. The root contributes the initial
match run. Each later block contributes its stored edit followed by its
match run. The chain has at most $\min\{k,n\}$ positive edits.
Expanding all match runs takes $O(n)$ time.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Cost truncation.}\label{sec:truncation}
For the implementation, run the frontier with
$w_k(a,b)=\min\{w(a,b),k+1\}$.
Uniform truncation at any $C>0$ preserves a metric because
$\min\{x+y,C\}\le\min\{x,C\}+\min\{y,C\}$ for $x,y\ge0$.
The original and truncated metrics have the same alignments of cost
below $C$. Each such alignment has the same cost under both metrics
because it cannot use an edge truncated to $C$.
Hence $w_k$ gives the same bounded answer as $w$.
Compute gap costs and prefix masses under $w_k$.
Prefix masses are at most $n(k+1)$.
The frontier integers therefore use
$O(\log(n+k+2))$ bits.

\subsection{Without a supplied threshold}\label{sec:exact-distance-sensitive}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:exact-distance-sensitive}
Let $D=\WED_w(X,Y)$ for an integer metric $w$.
The distance and an optimal alignment can be computed in
$O(n+\min\{D^2,n^2\})$ time and space.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For equal strings, return zero and the match alignment. Otherwise,
preprocess once using the fixed metric $w_n$.
Run the frontier with thresholds $1,2,4,\ldots$ below $n$.
Then run it with threshold $n$. Stop at the first success.
If all calls fail, use
the full dynamic program under $w$.
For $0<D\le n$, the first successful threshold is at most $2D$.
The squared thresholds sum to $O(D^2)$. For $D>n$, the failed
frontiers and the dynamic program take $O(n^2)$ time. Release each
failed frontier before the next call and reconstruct the alignment once.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Weaker substitution conditions}\label{prop:gap-compatible}

The bounded frontier also computes minimum alignment cost under weaker
conditions than a metric. Suppose insertion and deletion of $a$ both
cost a positive integer $g(a)$. Write $t(a,b)$ for the substitution
cost and require $t(a,a)=0$. For distinct symbols $a,b$, the cost
$t(a,b)$ must be a positive integer satisfying
\[
 |g(a)-g(b)|\le t(a,b)\le g(a)+g(b).
\]
These inequalities imply the mass-potential and savings bounds.
Prefix removal, common-prefix cancellation, and the frontier
correctness proof therefore apply without change.
The algorithm uses $O(n+k^2)$ time and space.
Neither symmetry nor a substitution triangle inequality is needed.
The conclusion concerns minimum alignment cost.
An unrestricted edit sequence can have lower cost by replacing a
symbol in several steps through intermediate symbols.

\section{Rounding and gap lattices}\label{sec:approximation}\label{sec:extensions}

\subsection{Approximation by upward rounding}

Let $w$ be a normalized real metric and let $0<\eps\le1$.
Choose a power of two $Q$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
 1/\eps\le Q<2/\eps.
\end{equation}
Round the scaled costs upward by setting
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding}
 w_Q(a,b)=\lceil Qw(a,b)\rceil.
\end{equation}
The rounded costs form an integer metric. The triangle inequality for
$w$ gives
\[
 \lceil Qw(a,c)\rceil
 \le \lceil Qw(a,b)\rceil+\lceil Qw(b,c)\rceil.
\]

Write $D=\WED_w(X,Y)$ and $D_Q=\WED_{w_Q}(X,Y)$.
Every alignment has rounded cost at least $Q$ times its original
cost. An optimal alignment for $w$ has at most $D$ positive edits
because each costs at least one. Rounding adds less than one per
positive edit. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding-guarantee}
 QD\le D_Q\le(Q+1)D.
\end{equation}
The estimate $A=D_Q/Q$ satisfies $D\le A\le(1+\eps)D$.
An optimal alignment for $w_Q$ has original cost at most $A$.
We can therefore use the integer frontier to compute the rounded
optimum.

\paragraph{Implementing rounded queries.}\label{sec:rounded-queries}
We compute capped rounded costs using exact comparisons. This avoids
requiring a ceiling operation in the computational model.
For an integer cap $C>0$, define
\[
 v_{Q,C}(a,b)=\min\{w_Q(a,b),C\}.
\]
The \hyperref[sec:truncation]{uniform truncation argument} shows
that $v_{Q,C}$ is a metric. It gives the same bounded answers as
$w_Q$ for every budget $B<C$.

To query $v_{Q,C}(a,b)$, return zero if $a=b$. Otherwise, query
$z=w(a,b)$ and set $q=0$. Process the powers $1,2,4,\ldots$ up to
$C$ in decreasing order. Add a power $h$ to $q$ if $q+h\le C$ and
$q+h<Qz$. Return $\min\{q+1,C\}$. The final $q$ is the largest integer in $[0,C]$
strictly below $Qz$. The returned value is therefore
$\min\{\lceil Qz\rceil,C\}$. Generating the powers of two and making
the comparisons takes $O(\log(C+2))$ exact arithmetic operations.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:approximation}]
Set $B=(Q+1)k$ and $C=B+1$. Run the integer frontier on $v_{Q,C}$
with budget $B$. The cap preserves all alignment costs at most $B$.
Thus a successful call returns $D_Q$. Return $A=D_Q/Q$ and the
recovered alignment. Their approximation guarantee follows
from~\eqref{eq:rounding-guarantee}. A failed call gives
$D_Q>B=(Q+1)k$. Equation~\eqref{eq:rounding-guarantee} then implies $D>k$.
If $D\le k$, then $D_Q\le B$ and the call succeeds.

Preprocessing and the frontier use $O(n+B^2)$ operations and queries
to $v_{Q,C}$. Each such query uses $O(1)$ queries to $w$ and
$O(\log(C+2))$ exact operations. Choosing $Q$ by doubling takes
$O(\log(1/\eps+2))$ operations. Since $B=O(k/\eps)$, the total time and space are
$\softO(n+(k/\eps)^2)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gap lattices}

The frontier bound depends on the number of possible scores and mass
offsets. These can have different spacings.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:lattices}
Let $\eta>0$ and $\gamma>0$ be known spacings. Suppose $w$ is a metric
whose edit costs are integer multiples of $\eta$ and whose gap costs
are positive integer multiples of $\gamma$.
For any threshold $k\ge0$, the exact bounded problem can be solved
in
\[
 O\bigl(n+(1+k/\eta)(1+k/\gamma)\bigr)
\]
time and space. On success, the algorithm also returns an optimal
alignment. The bound assumes constant-time exact arithmetic on
lattice indices.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Scores are multiples of $\eta$ and mass offsets are multiples of
$\gamma$. Store the entries as $R[c/\eta,d/\gamma]$ using these integer indices.
There are $O(1+k/\eta)$ score layers. At score $c$, the mass-potential
bound gives $|d|\le c\le k$. Each layer therefore has
$O(1+k/\gamma)$ offsets. The dominance rule, transitions, and
traceback are unchanged. The frontier queries $w$ directly.
An arbitrary cap need not preserve both lattices.
\end{proof}

For a normalized real metric with integer gap costs, rounding gives
$w_Q(a,\gap)=Qw(a,\gap)$. Scores remain integers and mass offsets
are multiples of $Q$. Use budget $B=(Q+1)k$ and cap
\[
 C=Q(2k+1)>B.
\]
The cap is a multiple of $Q$, so truncation preserves the gap lattice.
The compressed frontier has $O(Qk)$ score layers and $O(k)$ offsets
per layer. Proposition~\ref{prop:lattices} therefore gives
$O(n+Qk^2)$ frontier operations. The rounding guarantee and capped
queries give the following improvement.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:integer-gaps}
For normalized real metrics with integer gap costs, the bounded
$(1+\eps)$-approximation of Theorem~\ref{thm:approximation} uses
$\softO(n+k^2/\eps)$ time and space.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Without a supplied threshold}\label{sec:approx-distance-sensitive}

Both approximation algorithms reuse the LCE index and gap-mass
preprocessing across calls. Each uses one fixed capped metric and
doubles the budget parameter. We switch to exact dynamic programming
before a further call would exceed quadratic work.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:approx-distance-sensitive}
Let $w$ be a normalized real metric and let $0<\eps\le1$.
Write $D=\WED_w(X,Y)$.
A $(1+\eps)$-approximation and an alignment of cost at most its
estimate can be computed in
\[
 \softO\bigl(n+\min\{(D/\eps)^2,n^2\}\bigr)
\]
time and space. If the gap costs are integers,
the bound improves to
\begin{equation}\label{prop:integer-gap-distance-sensitive}
 \softO\bigl(n+\min\{D^2/\eps,n^2\}\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For equal strings, return zero and the match alignment. Otherwise,
normalization gives $D\ge1$.

For general gap costs, start with $Q=1$ and double it while $Q\eps<1$.
Switch to the exact dynamic program as soon as $Q>n$.
At this switch, the preceding power $Q/2$ was below $1/\eps$.
Hence $D/\eps>n/2$.
Quadratic work therefore satisfies the claimed bound. If $Q\le n$,
preprocess the capped metric $v_{Q,n+1}$ once. Run frontiers with
budgets equal to the powers of two below $n$. Then run a frontier
with budget $n$. At the first success, return $D_Q/Q$ and its
alignment. The first successful budget is at most $2D_Q$.
No tested budget exceeds $n$. The total frontier work is
\[
 O\bigl(n+\min\{D_Q^2,n^2\}\bigr)
 =O\bigl(n+\min\{(D/\eps)^2,n^2\}\bigr).
\]
If every call fails, then $D_Q>n$. Since
$D_Q\le(Q+1)D\le3D/\eps$, we have $D/\eps>n/3$.
The exact dynamic program again has the claimed cost.

For integer gap costs, use the same construction of $Q$.
Switch to exact dynamic programming as soon as $Q>n^2$.
At this switch, $D^2/\eps>n^2/2$.
Otherwise, set $C=Q(2n+1)$ and preprocess $v_{Q,C}$ once.
Try $k=1,2,4,\ldots$ while $Qk^2\le n^2$.
Each lattice frontier uses rounded score budget $B=(Q+1)k$.
Each tested $k$ is at most $n$, so $B<C$.
A failed call gives $D_Q>B$ and hence $D>k$. The first successful $k$ is
therefore at most $2D$. Summing the $O(Qk^2)$ frontier work gives
\[
 O\bigl(n+\min\{QD^2,n^2\}\bigr)
 =O\bigl(n+\min\{D^2/\eps,n^2\}\bigr).
\]
On success, return the rounded optimum divided by $Q$ and its
alignment. If all calls fail, let $L$ be the largest tested threshold.
The next threshold violates the work limit. Thus $QL^2>n^2/4$.
We also have $D>L$. These inequalities and $Q<2/\eps$ give
$D^2/\eps>n^2/8$. They therefore justify quadratic fallback.

In both variants, the cap and $Q$ are polynomially bounded in $n$.
Thus capped queries add only logarithmic factors in $n+2$.
Discarding failed frontiers gives the stated space bounds. The
algorithm reconstructs the alignment after a successful frontier call or during the
final dynamic program.
\end{proof}
