\begin{abstract}
\normalsize
We give a deterministic $O(n+k^2)$-time algorithm for bounded weighted
edit distance under an input integer metric on the alphabet extended
by a gap symbol. Here $n$ is the total input length and
$k$ is the supplied threshold. The algorithm returns the exact
distance and an optimal alignment if the distance is at most $k$. It reports that the
distance exceeds $k$ otherwise. The running time matches the classical
unit-cost bound and does not depend on the largest metric value.

The algorithm groups positions by the difference between their prefix
gap costs. We prove that removing prefixes of equal total gap cost
cannot increase the distance between the remaining suffixes. This
inequality justifies keeping one representative per score and mass
offset. For real metrics whose nonzero costs are at least one, upward
rounding gives a bounded $(1+\eps)$-approximation in
$\softO(n+(k/\eps)^2)$ time. Integer gap costs
improve this bound to $\softO(n+k^2/\eps)$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Edit distance is the minimum number of insertions, deletions, and
substitutions needed to transform one string into another. The classical
dynamic program takes quadratic time~\cite{WF74}. Greedy algorithms solve
the bounded problem in $O(n+k^2)$ time~\cite{Ukk85,Mye86,LV88}.
Here $n$ is the total input length and $k$ is a supplied threshold.
We focus on the regime $k\le n$.
Under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), unit-cost edit
distance has no strongly subquadratic algorithm~\cite{BI18,BK15}.

Weighted edit distance assigns costs to edits according to their symbols.
The dynamic program extends directly. The greedy frontier needs a
different dominance rule.
At each score, a unit-cost frontier keeps the farthest position on each
positional diagonal. Under symbol-dependent gap costs, a farther position
can leave a larger distance between the remaining suffixes. This can
happen even when the costs form a metric. Keeping only the farther
position can then discard the cheapest completion.

We use the known prefix-mass potential to prove an exact dominance rule
and obtain the $O(n+k^2)$ bound for integer metric costs. A \emph{mass
offset} is the difference between the total gap costs of the consumed prefixes.
Our main lemma shows that removing prefixes of equal total gap cost
cannot increase the remaining distance.
At each score and mass offset, the farthest generated position can
therefore replace all earlier candidates. The metric triangle inequality
bounds the absolute mass offset by the score. Integer costs give
$2c+1$ possible offsets at score $c$ and $O(k^2)$ frontier entries in total.

\subsection{Our results}\label{sec:results}

Let $X,Y\in\Sigma^*$ have total length $n$, and let
$\gap\notin\Sigma$ be a gap symbol. The input metric $w$ is defined
on $\Sigma\cup\{\gap\}$. Inserting or deleting $a$ costs
$w(a,\gap)$. A substitution from $a$ to $b$ costs $w(a,b)$.
The input symbols have polynomially bounded integer codes.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:integer}
Given an integer-valued metric $w$ and an integer threshold $k\ge1$,
a deterministic algorithm computes $\WED_w(X,Y)$ and an optimal
alignment if the distance is at most $k$. It reports that the distance
exceeds $k$ otherwise. The algorithm uses $O(n+k^2)$ time and space.
\end{theorem}

The bound is independent of the alphabet size and the largest metric
value.
We compare our bounds with Gorbachev and Kociumaka's bounds for general
weighted costs. We call a metric \emph{normalized}
if every nonzero cost is at least one. The notation $\softO$ suppresses
polylogarithmic factors.

Setting $k=n$ in the unit-cost case shows that the exponent $2$ is
optimal under SETH in the standard word-RAM model.

The frontier count uses integer scores and mass offsets. For normalized
real metrics, upward rounding restores this structure.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:approximation}
Let $w$ be a normalized real metric and let $k\ge1$ be an integer
threshold. For $0<\eps\le1$, a deterministic algorithm either correctly
reports that the distance exceeds $k$ or returns a value $A$ satisfying
\[
 \WED_w(X,Y)\le A\le(1+\eps)\WED_w(X,Y)
\]
together with an alignment of cost at most $A$.
The algorithm uses $\softO(n+(k/\eps)^2)$ time and space.
\end{theorem}

For normalized real metrics with integer insertion and deletion costs, the
approximation time improves to $\softO(n+k^2/\eps)$.
The substitution costs can remain nonintegral.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

For a fixed finite
alphabet and a fixed rational scoring function, Gorbachev and
Kociumaka~\cite{GK25} obtain quadratic dependence on $k$.
Our bound allows the integer metric to be part of the input.

For general normalized weighted costs, Das et al.~\cite{DGHKS23}
gave an $O(n+k^5)$-time algorithm. Cassis, Kociumaka, and
Wellnitz~\cite{CKW23} gave an exact
$\softO(n+\sqrt{nk^3})$-time algorithm for real costs. Their
lower-bound constructions include metric rational costs with
input-dependent granularity. Gorbachev and Kociumaka
gave the integer and approximation bounds in
Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.
Their algorithm for costs in $[0,W]$ uses a hierarchical alignment
data structure and core-sparse Monge matrices. The metric assumption
lets our algorithm use only a score frontier and longest common
extension queries.

Charalampopoulos, Kociumaka, and Wellnitz~\cite{CKW25} study pattern
matching under weighted edit distance, including metrics with bounded
integer costs. Boneh, Gorbachev, and Kociumaka~\cite{BGK25} give dynamic
algorithms and conditional lower bounds for bounded weighted edit
distance under general normalized costs.

Mader, Tavasoli, and Wang~\cite{MTW26} and Das, Kipouridis, and
Kociumaka~\cite{DKK26} give randomized strongly subquadratic
$(3+\eps)$-approximations for arbitrary metric weighted edit
distance. Both papers use prefix-mass potentials to restrict candidate
locations. Das et al. identify the prefix-mass difference as the weighted
analogue of a diagonal coordinate. We use this potential to justify
retaining only the farthest generated candidate at each score and mass
offset.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}

\label{sec:model}
We use a unit-cost RAM with constant-time metric queries, exact
arithmetic, and comparisons.

The alignment formulation and string-metric properties are
classical~\cite{WF74,Sel74}. We recall the prefix-mass potential
from~\cite{MTW26,DKK26}. Longest common
extensions use standard suffix-array and range-minimum
structures~\cite{KSB06,BF00}.

\paragraph{Strings and alignments.}
Let $m=|X|$, $r=|Y|$, and $n=m+r$.
Positions are zero-based. We write $Z[i,j)$ for a substring and
$Z[i:]=Z[i,|Z|)$ for a suffix. Write $g(a)=w(a,\gap)$ for the gap cost of $a$.
A match costs zero.

Weighted edit distance $\WED_w(A,B)$ is the minimum cost of an edit
sequence from $A$ to $B$. For a fixed metric, write
$D(A,B)=\WED_w(A,B)$ and $D=D(X,Y)$.
An alignment is a path from $(0,0)$ to $(|A|,|B|)$ in the edit grid.
Its edges are
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
 (i,j)\longrightarrow(i+1,j)
   &\text{at cost }g(A[i])&\text{(deletion)},\\
 (i,j)\longrightarrow(i,j+1)
   &\text{at cost }g(B[j])&\text{(insertion)},\\
 (i,j)\longrightarrow(i+1,j+1)
   &\text{at cost }w(A[i],B[j])&\text{(substitution or match)}.
\end{array}
\]

For metric costs, weighted edit distance equals the minimum alignment
cost.
Every subpath of an optimal alignment is optimal for its endpoints.

\paragraph{Gap masses.}
Define
\[
 \mu(Z)=\sum_{p=0}^{|Z|-1}g(Z[p]),\qquad
 \mu_Z(i)=\mu(Z[0,i)),\qquad
 \delta(i,j)=\mu_X(i)-\mu_Y(j).
\]
We call $\mu(Z)$ the \emph{gap mass} and $\delta(i,j)$ the
\emph{mass offset}. Prefix masses are strictly increasing because
every gap cost is positive.
The reverse triangle inequality gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gap-inequalities}
 |g(a)-g(b)|\le w(a,b).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:potential}
An alignment subpath from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$ of cost $c$ satisfies
\[
 |\delta(i',j')-\delta(i,j)|\le c.
\]
In particular, a path of cost $c$ from $(0,0)$ reaches only vertices
with $|\delta|\le c$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A deletion or insertion changes the mass offset by its signed gap
cost. A diagonal edge pairing $a,b$ changes it by $g(a)-g(b)$.
The absolute value of this change is at most $w(a,b)$
by~\eqref{eq:gap-inequalities}. Summing the absolute changes along
the subpath proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Longest common extensions.}\label{sec:lce}
Let $\lce(i,j)$ be the longest common prefix length of $X[i:]$ and
$Y[j:]$. Define
\[
 \Ext(i,j)=(i+\lce(i,j),j+\lce(i,j)).
\]
The symbol-code assumption gives deterministic $O(n)$-time and
$O(n)$-space preprocessing for constant-time LCE queries.


\section{Prefix-mass dominance}\label{sec:dominance}

Consider an alignment after it has consumed both removed prefixes
for the first time. It may already have consumed part of one suffix.
If the prefixes have equal gap mass, the potential bound shows that
the cost paid so far covers the insertion or deletion of this part.
We can then follow the remaining subpath to align the suffixes.
The same argument recovers the known common-prefix cancellation
property~\cite{CKW23}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:removal}
For all strings $P,Q,U,V$,
\[
 D(U,V)\le D(PU,QV)+|\mu(P)-\mu(Q)|.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Follow an optimal alignment of $PU$ and $QV$ to the first vertex
$(i,j)$ with $i\ge|P|$ and $j\ge|Q|$.
Each coordinate increases by at most one per edge, so
$i=|P|$ or $j=|Q|$.

Suppose $i=|P|$. The consumed strings are $P$ and $QR$ for a
prefix $R$ of $V$. Let $c$ be the cost of this initial subpath.
Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} gives
\[
 |\mu(P)-\mu(Q)-\mu(R)|\le c,
\]
and hence $\mu(R)\le c+|\mu(P)-\mu(Q)|$.

The remaining subpath aligns $U$ with $V[|R|:]$ at cost $D(PU,QV)-c$.
Insert $R$ at cost $\mu(R)$ and then follow this subpath.
This gives an alignment of $U$ with $V$ of cost at most
\[
 D(PU,QV)-c+\mu(R)
 \le D(PU,QV)+|\mu(P)-\mu(Q)|.
\]
When $j=|Q|$, the symmetric construction deletes a prefix of $U$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:dominance}
If $i_2\ge i_1$ and $\delta(i_2,j_2)=\delta(i_1,j_1)$, then
$j_2\ge j_1$ and
\[
 D(X[i_2:],Y[j_2:])\le D(X[i_1:],Y[j_1:]).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Equality of the offsets gives
$\mu_Y(j_2)-\mu_Y(j_1)=\mu_X(i_2)-\mu_X(i_1)\ge0$.
Since prefix masses strictly increase, $j_2\ge j_1$.
The intervening prefixes $X[i_1,i_2)$ and $Y[j_1,j_2)$ have equal
mass. Lemma~\ref{lem:removal} gives the distance inequality.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:cancellation}\label{lem:extension}
For all strings $P,U,V$, $D(PU,PV)=D(U,V)$.
Applying $\Ext$ therefore preserves both the mass offset and the
remaining distance.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:removal} gives $D(U,V)\le D(PU,PV)$.
Matching the two copies of $P$ for free gives the reverse inequality.
\end{proof}

\section{The bounded weighted frontier}\label{sec:integer}\label{sec:frontier}

The frontier processes prefix alignments by increasing score.
Before storing a generated candidate, it matches the longest common
prefix of the remaining suffixes.
Corollary~\ref{cor:cancellation} shows that this extension preserves
both the mass offset and the remaining distance.

For each integer score $0\le c\le k$ and mass offset $-c\le d\le c$,
the entry $R[c,d]$ stores a record for a vertex $(i,j)$ reached by an
alignment of cost exactly $c$ with $\delta(i,j)=d$.
Each entry retains the generated candidate with the largest
$X$-coordinate $i$ after match extension.
By Corollary~\ref{cor:dominance}, the retained candidate has the same
prefix cost and no larger remaining distance than a discarded candidate.
It therefore preserves an optimal completion whenever a discarded
candidate admits one.

From a stored vertex $(i,j)$, generate each valid insertion, deletion,
and substitution. Every generated edit has positive integer cost
because match extension has already consumed all immediate matches.
The destination therefore belongs to a later score layer. The
mass-potential bound in Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} places every
candidate of score $c$ within $[-c,c]$.

For traceback, each record also stores a predecessor entry $p$,
its last positive edit $e$, and the length $\ell$ of the following
match run.

The procedure $\Offer(c,i,j,p,e)$ discards a candidate if $c>k$.
Otherwise, it sets $\ell=\lce(i,j)$ and replaces $(i,j)$ by
$(i+\ell,j+\ell)$. It then computes $d=\delta(i,j)$.
The candidate is stored in $R[c,d]$ if the entry is empty or has a
smaller $X$-coordinate. The root stores its initial match run.
The frontier processes score layers in increasing
order and returns upon processing the first terminal record.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bounded weighted edit distance}\label{alg:frontier}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $X,Y$, integer metric $w$, threshold $k\ge1$, gap costs,
prefix masses, and LCE index
\Function{Frontier}{$k$}
  \State Initialize $R[c,d]$ as empty for $0\le c\le k$, $-c\le d\le c$
  \State \Call{Offer}{$0,0,0,\mathsf{null},\mathsf{null}$}
  \For{$c=0,1,\ldots,k$}
    \For{$d=-c,-c+1,\ldots,c$}
      \If{$R[c,d]$ is empty} \State \textbf{continue} \EndIf
      \State $(i,j)\gets(R[c,d].i,R[c,d].j)$
      \If{$(i,j)=(m,r)$}
        \State \Return $c$ and the alignment from the predecessor chain
      \EndIf
      \ForAll{outgoing edit edges $e:(i,j)\to(i',j')$ of cost $t$}
        \State \Call{Offer}{$c+t,i',j',(c,d),e$}
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\FAIL$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection{Correctness and running time}\label{sec:traceback}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:integer}]
Every stored record represents a prefix alignment of its stated score.
The root matches a common prefix. Each later record appends one edit
and a match run to its predecessor's alignment.
Suppose $D\le k$. Call a record at
score $c$ and vertex $(i,j)$ \emph{feasible} if
\[
 c+D(X[i:],Y[j:])\le D.
\]
A feasible record can be completed to an optimal alignment.
We track the existence of such a record as the score layers are
processed. A replacement may change its prefix alignment and endpoint.
The initial record is feasible by common-prefix cancellation.
Match extension preserves feasibility. Replacing a candidate by a later
vertex at the same score and mass offset also preserves feasibility
by Corollary~\ref{cor:dominance}.

When a feasible nonterminal record is processed, match extension has
left no immediate match.
An optimal suffix alignment therefore starts with a positive edit.
Let this edit have cost $t$ and end at $(i',j')$.
Then
\[
 t+D(X[i':],Y[j':])=D(X[i:],Y[j:]).
\]
The algorithm generates this edit at score $c+t\le D\le k$.
After match extension, retaining the farthest generated candidate
produces a feasible record in a later layer.
Subsequent replacements preserve feasibility until that layer is
processed. Following these feasible representatives gives strictly
increasing integer scores bounded by $D$. The sequence must therefore
reach a terminal record. Every terminal record realizes an alignment
and has score at least $D$. The first processed terminal score is
therefore $D$.
If $D>k$, no terminal record can occur in the processed layers.

At score $c$ there are $2c+1$ possible offsets.
The frontier therefore has $O(k^2)$ entries.
Each entry is processed once and generates at most
three candidates. Each candidate uses one LCE query and constant
additional work. Gap costs, prefix masses, and the LCE index take
$O(n)$ preprocessing time and space. Thus the total time and space
are $O(n+k^2)$.

Positive edits increase the score, so a layer is final before it is
processed. Its records cannot change after they generate children.
The predecessor pointers therefore remain valid.
Follow the terminal record's predecessor chain and reverse its blocks
$(e,\ell)$ to obtain the alignment. The root contributes the initial
match run. Each later block contributes its stored edit followed by its
match run. The chain has at most $k$ positive edits.
Expanding all match runs takes $O(n)$ time.
\end{proof}

\section{Real costs}\label{sec:approximation}\label{sec:extensions}

Upward rounding lets us apply the integer frontier to normalized real
metrics. Integer gap costs give fewer mass offsets after rounding.

\subsection{Upward rounding}

Let $w$ be a normalized real metric and let $0<\eps\le1$.
Choose a power of two $Q$ by doubling until
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
 1/\eps\le Q<2/\eps.
\end{equation}
Round the scaled costs upward by setting
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding}
 w_Q(a,b)=\lceil Qw(a,b)\rceil.
\end{equation}
The rounded costs form an integer metric. The triangle inequality for
$w$ gives
\[
 \lceil Qw(a,c)\rceil
 \le \lceil Qw(a,b)\rceil+\lceil Qw(b,c)\rceil.
\]

Write $D=\WED_w(X,Y)$ and $D_Q=\WED_{w_Q}(X,Y)$.
Every alignment has rounded cost at least $Q$ times its original cost.
An optimal alignment for $w$ has at most $D$ positive edits because
each costs at least one. Rounding adds less than one per positive edit.
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rounding-guarantee}
 QD\le D_Q\le(Q+1)D.
\end{equation}
The estimate $A=D_Q/Q$ satisfies $D\le A\le(1+\eps)D$.
An optimal alignment for $w_Q$ has original cost at most $A$.

The frontier only uses alignments within its budget.
We cap larger costs so that rounded queries can be evaluated by
searching a bounded range of integers.
For an integer cap $C>0$, define
\[
 v_{Q,C}(a,b)=\min\{w_Q(a,b),C\}.
\]
Capping preserves the triangle inequality because
$\min\{x+y,C\}\le\min\{x,C\}+\min\{y,C\}$ for $x,y\ge0$.
An alignment of cost below $C$ under either metric has the same cost
under both because it cannot use an edge capped to $C$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:approximation}]
Set $B=(Q+1)k$ and $C=B+1$. Run the integer frontier on $v_{Q,C}$
with budget $B$. Since $B<C$, a successful call returns the uncapped
distance $D_Q$. We return $D_Q/Q$ and the recovered alignment.
Their approximation guarantee follows
from~\eqref{eq:rounding-guarantee}. A failed call gives $D_Q>(Q+1)k$
and hence $D>k$. If $D\le k$, then $D_Q\le B$ and the call succeeds.

The frontier and preprocessing use $O(n+B^2)$ operations and queries.
Each capped query takes $O(\log C)$ exact operations.
Choosing $Q$ takes $O(\log C)$ doublings.
Since $B=O(k/\eps)$, the total time and space are
$\softO(n+(k/\eps)^2)$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Rounded queries.}\label{sec:rounded-queries}
We evaluate $v_{Q,C}$ by exact comparisons. For $a=b$, return zero.
Otherwise, query $z=w(a,b)$ and start with $q=0$.

Scan powers of two $h$ from the largest at most $C$ down to $1$.
Add $h$ to $q$ when $q+h\le C$ and $q+h<Qz$.
The final $q$ is the largest integer in $[0,C]$ strictly below $Qz$.

Return $\min\{q+1,C\}$, which equals $\min\{\lceil Qz\rceil,C\}$.
The search takes $O(\log C)$ exact operations.

\subsection{Integer gap costs}

Suppose the original gap costs are integers. Then
$w_Q(a,\gap)=Qw(a,\gap)$, so every mass offset is a multiple of $Q$.
The score layers remain integral. Iterating only over these offsets
reduces the frontier size by a factor of $Q$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:integer-gaps}
For normalized real metrics with integer gap costs, the bounded
$(1+\eps)$-approximation of Theorem~\ref{thm:approximation} uses
$\softO(n+k^2/\eps)$ time and space.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use budget $B=(Q+1)k$.
To preserve the spacing of gap costs, choose the cap $C=Q(2k+1)>B$.
The cap is a multiple of $Q$, so all capped gap costs and mass offsets
remain multiples of $Q$.
Store the frontier entries as $R[c,d/Q]$.

There are $B+1=O(Qk)$ score layers.
In each layer, Lemma~\ref{lem:potential} gives $|d|\le B$.
The spacing $Q$ leaves at most $2B/Q+1=O(k)$ possible offsets.
The frontier therefore uses $O(n+Qk^2)$ operations and capped queries.

Equation~\eqref{eq:rounding-guarantee} gives the same approximation
and threshold guarantees. The capped-query overhead is logarithmic,
and $Q=O(1/\eps)$ gives the stated bound.
\end{proof}
